## 💡Wording tweaks

### (1) “Would you be open to…” instead of Can you…”

**Example:** “Would you be open to writing a small 1-pager so that we can align on the path forward?”

This works because most people consider themselves “open-minded.” So when you ask, “Would you be open to…” there’s a connection to that sense of being open-minded.

“Can you” works too. It’s just more of a direct request.

“Would you be willing to” is the worst because it makes it seem like it’s a big ask with the word “willing.”

[_Source: Exactly What to Say by Phil M Jones_](https://www.amazon.com/Exactly-What-Say-Influence-Impact-ebook/dp/B073SF65ZZ)

### **(2) Adding “because” to any request**

There’s a famous study done by social psychologist Ellen Langer. Here is a snippet of it from [this site](https://execvision.io/blog/power-because/#:~:text=Social%20psychologist%20Ellen%20Langer%20demonstrated,resulted%20in%20near%2Dtotal%20effectiveness.).

> Social psychologist Ellen Langer demonstrated this unsurprising fact with a simple experiment. She asked a small favor of people waiting in line to use a library photocopier: Excuse me, I have five pages. May I use the Xerox machine because I’m in a rush?

The request-plus-reason resulted in near-total effectiveness. 94% of people Dr. Langer asked let her skip ahead of them in line.

Compare this to the success rate of the request alone: _Excuse me, I have five pages. May I use the Xerox machine? Only 60% of those asked let her cut in line in this instance._

94% effectiveness when adding “because” compared to 60% when not using it. Even when the reason attached to the “because” isn’t even that strong.

### **(3) “Can we…” instead of “Can you…”**

It seems weird, but using “we” in requests, particularly in code reviews, gives a more collaborative feeling and comes off as less commanding.

**Example:** “Can we rename this to match the pattern in the other file?”

### **(4) “What do you think?” at the end of your suggestion**

I learned this one from the [influence article](https://careercutler.substack.com/p/influence-lessons-from-a-staff-engineer) we did with 8-year Twitter Tech Lead and Staff Engineer, Ron DeVera.

Asking “What do you think” after a suggestion allows you to explain what you think is best while leaving the discussion open.

**Example: “**I was thinking we should move standup to every other day because meeting every day takes up a good amount of time to code for me. What do you think?”

### **(5) “It seems like” for stalemates in conversation.**

Chris Voss talks about this concept in his book, [Never Split the Difference](https://www.amazon.com/Never-Split-Difference-Negotiating-Depended-ebook/dp/B014DUR7L2).

It’s called **labeling**.

To learn more about it, check this [30-second video](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/36dbbCogm4M) or this [3-minute video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ThQ8xPGg-QQ)

I use labeling when it seems like there’s no path forward in a conversation.

**Let’s look at an example:**

I was chatting with a teammate who seemed resistant to making a change to a team process. I said, “Ah, so **it seems like it would be impossible to make a change to the way we do this**.”

> Chris emphasizes that labeling a negative diffuses a negative.

In this case, “it would be impossible” is the negative. So me labeling that diffuses that negative and causes the other person to disagree with that.

So in this case the other person said, “Well, no. It’s not impossible. We could probably do…” and then made a suggestion that wasn’t on the table before. We moved forward with it and we were good to go!

### **(6) Changing the order of “but”**

Take these two statements:

- “I see what you're saying, **but** in my experience, the other way has worked best.”
- “In my experience, the other way has worked best, **but** I see what you're saying.”

The first one negates “I see what you’re saying.”

The second one negates “In my experience, the other way has worked best.”

When you use “but”, it’s important to understand which part you are negating.

To give a more collaborative vibe, you generally want to negate your own part rather than theirs since it risks them getting defensive. As soon as they get defensive, you’ll have a much harder time winning them over.

Another alternative is swapping your “but” for “at the same time” to emphasize that the two things can go together.

**Example: “**I see what you’re saying. At the same time, in my experience the other way has worked well for these reasons.”

## 📖 TL;DR

1. Use “Would you be open to” instead of “Can you” when you want to seem less commanding but still lead to a “yes.”
2. Add “because” to your reasoning or request to strengthen it.
3. Use “can we” instead of “can you” to be more collaborative, particularly in code reviews.
4. Use “What do you think” to assert a suggestion but still leave it open for discussion.
5. Use “It seems like” when the conversation is at a stalemate and you want to call it out directly. Many times this breaks the stalemate.
6. Change the order of your “but” to negate the part you actually want to negate.
