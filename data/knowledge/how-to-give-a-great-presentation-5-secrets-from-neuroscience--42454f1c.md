Okay, so you have to give a presentation at work…

First of all, I’m sorry. I truly am. Public speaking is a punishment Dante forgot to include in “Inferno” because he lacked imagination.

The thing I find weird is that people think presentations are terrifying because everyone’s staring at you. Wrong. They’re terrifying because _nobody_ is. The truly humiliating thing is that nobody cares.

Everybody in your audience has somewhere else they’d rather be. It’s like trying to perform “Hamlet” in front of a group of people who thought they were going to see a monster truck rally.

But you still want to give a great presentation. So what’s the secret?

Well, here’s where neuroscience comes in. Yeah, neuroscience, that smug, overachieving cousin of regular science. If you know how the brain works, maybe you can get your hostages audience to actually pay attention and learn something.

So let’s find out what neuroscience can teach us about giving a great presentation.

John Medina is a professor of bioengineering at the University of Washington School of Medicine. His wonderful book is “[Brain Rules.](https://geni.us/medinabrain)”

Let’s get to it…

## How To Start A Presentation: Lead With The Gist

Many people think presenting is merely conveying information.

_“I’ll just present the data as written and they’ll follow along.”_

Sure. And people watch celebrity breakups for sociological research.

Neuroscience research suggests something every good book and movie knows: if you don’t grab your audience in the opening moments, you will spend the rest of the hour speaking to a room of semi-conscious husks.

Medina is blunt: _“If you are trying to get information across to someone, a compelling introduction may be the most important single factor in the success of your mission.”_

So start with the gist of your talk. The big picture. The “Why should I care?” moment. None of that meandering preamble nonsense. Right out of the gate, punch them in the brain with your main point.

Medina says this provides a 40% boost in understanding. Without an early look at the big picture, people’s brains are left scrambling for coherence. You’re handing them puzzle pieces without showing them the box.

Then, present the information in a logical, hierarchical format. Key idea, details, next key idea. You want there to be an underlying structure they can follow, like a song, so you avoid the dreaded “Where is this going?” question.

Okay, you’ve got their brains engaged. But now there’s a new problem we’re all too familiar with: attention spans…

## The 10-Minute Rule: How To Keep An Audience’s Attention

A presentation is like a bank heist. You are on the clock from the moment you begin.

You have mere seconds to capture their attention. Accomplish that one? You’re _still_ on borrowed time. Psychologist Wilbert McKeachie found that audience attention to a speaker consistently nosedives after 10 minutes.

So when composing your presentation, divide it into 10-minute chunks with a “hook” after every section to yank your audience back from the brink of unconsciousness and reset the clock.

Each hook is 1-2 minutes dealing with a single core concept. Use the other 8-9 minutes to dive into the details explaining it. Repeat.

What do the best hooks have in common? Four things:

1. **Emotion**: Not optional. If you’re not moving people emotionally, you’re delivering verbal Valium. Humor counts as an emotion and thank God for that. 2. **Relevance**: If you just make a joke or tell a story about a lost puppy every ten minutes, the talk won’t cohere. The hook needs to be related. (Okay, maybe I won’t make the bidet joke.)
3. **Brevity**: Hooks, done well, are powerful. But this can threaten to overwhelm what the talk is actually about. Two minutes, max.
4. **Storytelling**: A short, relevant, emotional story is an excellent hook.

## Make Them Understand Or They Won’t Remember

Emotion is vital, and it’s the part everyone loves to talk about. But unless you’re a motivational speaker, it’s secondary. Emotion is the DJ at a strip club. Plays an important part, sure, but it’s not really why anybody showed up.

You need to make them _understand_ whatever it is your talk is about. If they don’t get it, they won’t remember it.

The secret? First, you must really understand the subject. Inside and out. You need to know why those numbers matter, what they mean, and how to explain them to someone who’s been actively avoiding numbers since high school.

A lovely side effect of being this prepared? Knowing your stuff cold is also where confidence comes from.

If you ever see me looking confident in public, please know one of two things is happening: I have dissociated completely, or I have to poop so badly that fear has been replaced by purpose. And before you say “Wow, that sounds like anxiety,” yes. Thank you. I’m aware. I live here.

But I’m extremely confident when presenting because I know the subject frighteningly well.

Which leads us to the _other_ common problem: because you understand it, you assume everyone else will, too. This is so prevalent it has a name: “the curse of knowledge,” which sounds like a bad Harry Potter spell.

You can’t just show people a bunch of numbers and hope for the best. Tell them what those numbers mean for their actual day-to-day lives. How does it connect to what they already know?

_“This results in an adjusted equivalency factor of 15% this quarter.”_

Uh… should I be happy? Scared? Should I order a cake?

It’s your job to connect the dots, and explain why this matters. You need to be the Bob Ross of corporate presentations, painting little trees of meaning all over your slide deck.

Your audience is not going to do that work for you. Paint the picture. Show them where the squirrels live.

## Use Examples To Make It Memorable

If you want people to remember the things you’re presenting, use lots of examples. Without examples, you’re asking people to _work_. You think anyone wants to work during your presentation? HAHAHAHAHAHA. No.

Our brains are lazy. Brains love examples because examples do half the thinking for them. Using rich examples and connecting your info to stuff people already know produces “elaborate encoding” which increases remembering.

Nobody remembers the bullet points or the pie charts. They remember the stories and the absurd examples that make the abstract concrete.

People do love statistics they can share. And these make you, the presenter, look intelligent without having to actually be intelligent. Trust me; I’ve built a career based on this principle.

Turn each number into a scandalous love triangle between profit, loss, and the sultry, unpredictable mistress known as market volatility.

## Use Visuals, Not Walls Of Text

Your gray matter remembers things better when multiple senses are involved. So incorporating images, audio, and video is neuroscience-approved.

Nearly half of your gray matter’s resources are devoted to processing visual information. Vision is, no doubt, the Freddie Mercury of the senses. It’s powerful and wears the tightest pants.

If possible, communicate more with pictures than with words. And if you dare use those sad stock photos of business people high-fiving in a conference room, I will personally attend your next presentation and heckle you into oblivion.

Don’t get too creative. We’ve all heard stories of someone using inappropriate images or videos, leading to them becoming famous in the worst possible way.

But there’s a reason that movies combine powerful visuals, great ideas, and music into the final product.

## Sum Up

Here’s how to give a great presentation:

- **How To Start A Presentation: Lead With The Gist:** You’re standing in front of a room full of people who’ve barely had enough coffee to make their faces function properly. Give them the gist up front and a structure they can follow.
- **The 10-Minute Rule: How To Keep An Audience’s Attention:** Assume you’re presenting for an audience of narcoleptics. Every ten minutes something notable needs to happen or they’re out.
- **Make Them Understand Or They Won’t Remember:** Do not be a fact parrot. If you drone on about KPIs without explaining what a KPI is, the audience will assume it means “Kill Presenter Immediately.”
- **Use Examples To Make It Memorable:** The human mind is not a sponge, despite what your kindergarten teacher claimed. It’s more like a sieve. Use rich, relatable stories to produce the lovely “Oh, _now_ I get it” response.
- **Use Visuals, Not Walls Of Text:** Are you putting up a slide that’s a wall of text? Then you deserve the collective groan you’re going to hear.

So how does the brain like you to prepare? It’s called state-dependent learning. Simply put: our ability to remember information is dramatically improved when we learn it in the same context we’re expected to recall it.

To the best of your ability, practice under the conditions in which you’ll present. Same amount of sleep, same amount of caffeine. Can you practice in front of a live person? Can you actually practice in the room you’ll be presenting in?

Recreate the vibe. Our brains are like method actors who insist on living in a cabin in the woods to really “get into the role” of a serial killer.

Can I offer one more tip, from lil ol’ me?

A lot of people think they need to seem impressive. Authoritative. This is an exhausting ambition. It also makes speaking much harder. You’re trying to communicate an idea while simultaneously operating a life-size puppet of yourself. No wonder you’ve forgotten what the next slide says.

I understand the impulse. I’d love to be effortlessly commanding. But think about someone you’ve actually enjoyed listening to. Chances are you remember their enthusiasm. A story they told.

Once you’ve done the hard work, once all the boxes are checked, be human. Once you know the material cold and you’ve replayed the talk over and over in your head like it’s the Zapruder film, focus on the passion and enthusiasm. Bring back the strip club DJ.

You’re allowed to enjoy the thing you’re talking about. It’s cool to care enough that it shows. Once you know your stuff, unguarded enthusiasm is welcome. And infectious.

Make them glad you came. Then leave before they’re glad you’re going.
